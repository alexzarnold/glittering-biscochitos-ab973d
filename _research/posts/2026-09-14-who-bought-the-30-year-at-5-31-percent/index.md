## The takeaway

On September 10 the Treasury sold \$22 billion of 30-year bonds at a yield of 5.308%, the highest clearing yield of any 30-year auction since at least 2009. Primary dealers, the banks that stand ready to absorb whatever other bidders do not take, were awarded 2.21% of the competitive bids. That is the lowest share in 211 nominal 30-year auctions going back to February 2009. The 10-year auction a day earlier showed the same pattern. At these yields, end buyers took nearly all of the long end of the curve themselves.

## The setup

All figures are from the U.S. Treasury's auction results (Fiscal Data, auctions query), as of September 11 2026.

This was a reopening, meaning the Treasury sold more of the 30-year bond it first issued in August, maturing August 2056. The results:

| | Sep 10 2026 |
|---|---|
| Offering | \$22.0 billion |
| High yield (the clearing yield) | 5.308% |
| Bid-to-cover ratio | 2.61 |
| Primary dealers | 2.21% of competitive awards |
| Direct bidders | 18.31% |
| Indirect bidders | 79.48% |

The bid-to-cover ratio, total bids divided by the amount sold, was 2.61. That is above the 2025 median of 2.38 and higher than 84% of nominal 30-year auctions since 2009. The indirect share of 79.48% was the second highest in the series, behind 80.47% on October 10 2024.

The dealer number is the one that stands out. Dealers bid for \$28.31 billion of bonds. They were awarded \$0.48 billion, about 1.7% of what they asked for. The previous low for their share was 5.88% in February of this year. The median from 2009 through 2025 was 28.0%.

It was not a one-off quirk of the 30-year. At the 10-year auction on September 9, dealers were awarded 4.31%, the second lowest of 215 nominal 10-year auctions since 2009. The only lower one was September 10 2025, at 4.21%.

The chart also shows something that has to be said plainly. Dealer share has been falling for more than a decade, from around half of each auction in 2012 to about 11% in the year before this one. Part of what happened on September 10 is a long trend reaching a new low. It was still an outlier against that trend: the median of the 12 auctions before it was 11.3%.

## The mechanism

Treasury auctions are single-price auctions. Bidders submit the lowest yield they will accept and how much they want. Treasury sorts the bids from lowest yield to highest and fills them in that order until the offering is covered. The yield on the last bid needed, the high yield, is the yield everyone who is awarded bonds receives.

Three kinds of bidder show up:

- **Primary dealers** are the 26 banks and broker-dealers that trade directly with the New York Fed. The New York Fed expects them "to bid on a pro-rata basis in all Treasury auctions at reasonably competitive prices." That makes them the backstop.
- **Direct bidders** submit bids for their own account without going through a dealer.
- **Indirect bidders** submit through a dealer or another intermediary. This group includes foreign central banks bidding through the New York Fed, but also asset managers and other clients whose bids a dealer passes along.

Because dealers bid for their full pro-rata share whether or not they want the bonds, much of that bidding sits at yields where they would be comfortable holding inventory, not at the most aggressive level. Their award works like a residual:

$$
\text{dealer award} \approx \text{offering} - \text{end-buyer demand at yields} \le y^{*}
$$

where $y^{*}$ is the high yield. If direct and indirect bidders want enough bonds at yields below the dealers' bids, the auction clears before it reaches the dealer bids and dealers get very little. A small dealer share means demand was deep below the clearing yield.

This matters after the auction too. Bonds a dealer is awarded are inventory. The dealer has to hedge them and then sell them on to end investors in the days that follow, and that selling can weigh on prices. When dealers take a quarter of an auction, the market has to absorb that supply later. When they take 2%, it already has.

What the data cannot tell you is exactly who the indirect bidders were. Treasury's auction results give the three categories and nothing finer. So the honest answer to the headline question is: not the dealers, mostly indirect bidders, and the auction results do not say who those were.

The yield itself is the most likely draw. A 5.31% yield on a 30-year bond is attractive to buyers that need long-dated income to match long-dated obligations, such as pension funds and insurers. But that is an inference from who typically buys long bonds, not something this data shows.

## Risks and what would change the view

**One auction is one auction.** Dealer share bounces around, and the chart shows single auctions well below the trend line that did not repeat. October's 30-year reopening will show whether this was a new level or a single strong day.

**Reopenings can run a little lighter on dealers.** From 2016 through 2025, the median dealer share at 30-year reopenings was 18.6%, against 21.5% for new issues. That gap is far too small to explain 2.21%, but it points the same way.

**Demand at 5.31% is not demand at any price.** The same auction that shows strong demand also cleared at the highest 30-year yield in this data. If yields fall back and dealer share climbs back toward its recent range, the right reading is that buyers came for the yield, not for the bond. If dealer shares stay near record lows as yields fall, the demand is deeper than that, and the long end of the curve has more natural buyers than the recent rise in yields would suggest.
