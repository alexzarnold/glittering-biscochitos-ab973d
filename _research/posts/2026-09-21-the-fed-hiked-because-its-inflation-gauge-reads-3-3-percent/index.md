## The takeaway

On September 16 the Federal Reserve raised its target range by a quarter point to 3.75 to 4.00%, its first hike since July 2023. If you track core CPI, which was 2.45% in August, that looks like a central bank overreacting to oil. The Fed does not target CPI. It targets PCE inflation, and core PCE was 3.34% in July, at or above 3% every month this year. On the Fed's own gauge there is no oil shock to look through. There is a core inflation problem, and a policy rate that was still below the Fed's own estimate of neutral.

## The setup

The statement was short. The vote was 12 to 0. "Inflation remains elevated. Today's policy action will support a timelier return to the Committee's 2 percent goal." It noted that "uncertainty remains elevated owing, in part, to geopolitical developments." (Federal Reserve, September 16 2026.)

The projections released the same day matter more than the statement. The median official now expects the funds rate to end 2026 at 4.1%, up from 3.8% in June. With the range now centered at 3.875%, that implies one more quarter-point hike this year. The median also projects core PCE inflation of 3.4% for 2026 and 2.5% for 2027, with unemployment at 4.1%. (Federal Reserve, Summary of Economic Projections, September 2026.)

The two inflation gauges the public watches tell very different stories:

| Measure | Latest | Change from a year earlier |
|---|---|---|
| Headline CPI | August | 3.35% |
| Core CPI (excluding food and energy) | August | 2.45% |
| Headline PCE | July | 3.70% |
| Core PCE | July | 3.34% |

Sources: Bureau of Labor Statistics and Bureau of Economic Analysis via FRED (CPIAUCSL, CPILFESL, PCEPI, PCEPILFE), as of September 18 2026.

This gap is new. Through September 2025, core CPI ran *above* core PCE. The lines crossed late last year and have moved apart since.

## The mechanism

**Why a central bank might look through an oil shock.** A jump in oil prices raises the price level and slows the economy at the same time. Raising rates does not produce more oil. It adds a second drag on growth on top of the first. So the standard advice is to look through the first-round effect on energy prices, as long as two things hold: underlying inflation stays near target, and long-run inflation expectations stay anchored. Two weeks ago we showed that the second condition holds. The 5y5y forward breakeven barely moved as oil spiked. On core CPI, the first condition looks fine too.

**Why the Fed did not.** On the Fed's gauge, the first condition fails. Core PCE has been at or above 3% every month of 2026, and its six-month annualized rate through July was 3.46%. That is not energy leaking into core with a lag. It is core inflation running a point and a third above target.

**Why the two gauges disagree.** They measure different baskets in different ways:

- *Scope.* CPI covers what urban households pay out of pocket. PCE covers everything consumed by households, including spending made on their behalf, such as employer-paid health insurance and Medicare.
- *Weights.* Shelter carries a much larger weight in CPI than in PCE, so housing costs pull the two apart whenever they diverge from everything else.
- *Formula.* PCE uses a chained formula that updates for how people substitute between goods. CPI adjusts more slowly.

One contributor is visible in the data. The PCE price index for financial services and insurance rose 7.59% in the year to the second quarter of 2026, up from 4.84% a year earlier (BEA via FRED, DIFSRG3Q086SBEA). That category includes fees charged as a share of assets, which rise when stock prices rise, and it barely registers in CPI. The free data does not allow a full breakdown of the gap, so this is one visible piece, not the whole explanation.

**How loose was policy?** A rough measure of how tight policy is compares the real policy rate, the nominal rate minus inflation, with the Fed's estimate of neutral:

$$
r = i - \pi
$$

Before the hike, with the range centered at 3.625% and core PCE at 3.34%, the real policy rate was about 0.29%. The Fed's median longer-run funds rate is 3.2%, and with a 2% inflation goal that implies a neutral real rate of about 1.2%. So before September 16, policy was well below neutral by the Fed's own numbers. After the hike, the real rate is about 0.54%. That is less loose, not tight. The projected path gets there only if inflation cooperates: a 4.1% funds rate against the projected 2.5% core PCE for 2027 gives a real rate of about 1.6%.

This measure uses past inflation where the forward-looking version would use expected inflation. It is a rough guide, not a precise reading.

## Risks and what would change the view

**Part of the gap may be tied to asset prices.** If financial services prices are a large share of what is keeping core PCE high, a fall in stock prices would pull core PCE down quickly, with no change in the underlying pressures the Fed cares about. In that case the hike will look like a response to a measurement quirk.

**The labor market is less solid than the statement says.** August payrolls rose 162,000, but that came after gains of 21,000 in July and 31,000 in June (BLS, September 4 2026 release). If the August number is revised down and unemployment moves up from 4.1%, the case for a second hike weakens fast.

**Oil could fall.** Brent was \$119.66 on September 18. A reversal would lower headline inflation, though this piece argues the Fed is reacting to core.

**The next checks are close.** August PCE is due at the end of this month. A core PCE reading that holds above 3.3% supports the hike and makes the projected second hike this year more likely. A clear drop in core PCE, especially one driven by financial services, would mean the gap between the two gauges was doing more of the work than the Fed's statement lets on.
